# 12. Medicines for Epilepsy

Most people who are told they have epilepsy leave the appointment with a prescription and a
lot of questions. This chapter answers the ones families ask most. It does not tell you which
medicine to take or how much. That belongs to your doctor.

!!! warning "Read this first"
    This is general information, not medical advice. We give no doses and no recommendations.
    Never start, stop, skip, or change a seizure medicine on your own, and never change one
    because of something you read here. This page was written by students from public sources
    and has not yet been reviewed by a clinician or the Epilepsy Foundation of Minnesota. We
    are seeking that review. In an emergency, call 911.

## What these medicines do
Seizure medicines are called antiseizure medicines, or ASMs. Older writing calls them
antiepileptic drugs or anticonvulsants. They do not cure epilepsy. They make seizures less
likely to start and less likely to spread.

A seizure begins when brain cells fire together too fast. Every ASM works by slowing some part
of that process. Different drugs aim at different targets:

- Sodium channel blockers slow the rapid firing of brain cells. Examples: carbamazepine,
  oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, lacosamide.
- Calcium channel blockers quiet a rhythm in the brain linked to absence seizures. Example:
  ethosuximide.
- Drugs that boost GABA, the brain's main calming signal. Examples: clobazam, diazepam,
  phenobarbital, vigabatrin.
- Drugs that act on SV2A, a protein that controls how brain cells release their signals.
  Examples: levetiracetam, brivaracetam.
- Drugs that block glutamate, the brain's main excitatory signal. Example: perampanel.
- Drugs with several actions at once. Examples: valproate, topiramate, zonisamide,
  cannabidiol.

Your doctor picks from these based on your seizure type, your age, your other health
conditions, other medicines you take, and whether you might become pregnant. Two people with
epilepsy can be on completely different drugs and both be right.
([Sills & Rogawski, *Neuropharmacology* 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32120063/);
[NINDS](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/epilepsy-and-seizures);
[Epilepsy Foundation seizure medication list](https://www.epilepsy.com/tools-resources/seizure-medication-list))

## Why the first medicine does not always work
Nobody can tell in advance which drug will suit which person. So the first prescription is an
informed try, and it works for a lot of people but not for everyone.

A study that followed 1,795 people with newly diagnosed epilepsy for up to 30 years found:

| Which medicine | Share who became seizure free for at least a year |
|---|---|
| The first one tried | 45.7% |
| The second, after the first failed | another 11.6% |
| The third | another 4.1% |
| The fourth and beyond | about 1% or less each |

By the end of the study 63.7% were seizure free. About nine in ten of those got there on their
first or second medicine.
([Chen et al., *JAMA Neurology* 2018](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2666189))

Two things follow. If the first drug does not work, switching is normal and the second try is
worth real hope. If two proper trials have failed, that is called drug-resistant epilepsy, and
it is the point to ask about a referral to an epilepsy center rather than a fifth or sixth
drug. See [Tests and Diagnosis](../13-tests-and-diagnosis/index.md) for what those centers do.

Changing drugs is usually done by overlapping them. The new one is built up while the old one
comes down, so you are never left unprotected.

## Never stop or change a medicine suddenly
This is the one rule on this page we will say twice. Stopping an ASM abruptly can cause
seizures worse than the ones the drug was treating, including status epilepticus, a
seizure that does not stop on its own. It is a medical emergency.

FDA-approved labels say this directly. The lamotrigine label, for example, instructs that the
drug be reduced in steps over at least two weeks rather than stopped at once, unless safety
demands faster.
([FDA label, DailyMed](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=17a20462-d9d2-43ac-bcad-603e8cc76e3b))

The same caution applies when you run out. If the pharmacy cannot fill the prescription, that
is a same-day problem, not a wait-and-see problem. Ask about an emergency supply. Our
[pharmacy problem solver](../../sims/pharmacy-problem/index.md) walks through the options.

## Side effects that are common
Most side effects show up in the first few weeks and many fade. Common ones across the class
include:

- Sleepiness, tiredness, or trouble sleeping
- Dizziness or unsteadiness
- Double or blurry vision
- Nausea, upset stomach, or diarrhea
- Headache
- Trouble finding words, slowed thinking, or trouble concentrating
- Mood or behavior changes, including irritability
- Weight gain or weight loss

Each drug has its own pattern, and the same drug affects different people differently. Keep a
simple note of what you notice and when. A dose adjustment or a different drug often fixes it.
([Epilepsy Foundation, common concerns with epilepsy medication](https://www.epilepsy.com/treatment/medicines/common-concerns))

!!! danger "Call the doctor today, and go to an emergency room if it is severe"
    Some reactions are rare but serious. Do not wait for the next appointment, and do not stop
    the medicine on your own while you wait for an answer. Call.

    - Any new rash. Rash with fever, blisters, peeling skin, or sores in the mouth or eyes
      can be Stevens-Johnson syndrome, which is life threatening. Lamotrigine and carbamazepine
      carry boxed warnings about this.
    - Fever with swollen glands, rash, and feeling very unwell. This can be DRESS, a whole-body
      reaction that damages organs. In 2025 the FDA added a warning about DRESS to levetiracetam
      and clobazam.
    - Yellowing of the skin or eyes, repeated vomiting, severe stomach pain, unusual bruising or
      bleeding. These point to the liver or the pancreas. Valproate carries boxed warnings for
      both.
    - New or worsening thoughts of self-harm, depression, or a sharp change in mood or behavior.
      The FDA requires a warning about suicidal thoughts and behavior on antiseizure drug labels,
      based on pooled data from 199 trials.
    - Confusion, repeated vomiting, or more seizures than usual can signal low blood sodium,
      which some of these drugs cause.

    Sources: [FDA DRESS warning, levetiracetam and clobazam](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-safety-and-availability/fda-warns-rare-serious-drug-reaction-antiseizure-medicines-levetiracetam-keppra-keppra-xr-elepsia-xr);
    [lamotrigine label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=17a20462-d9d2-43ac-bcad-603e8cc76e3b);
    [carbamazepine label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=8d409411-aa9f-4f3a-a52c-fbcb0c3ec053);
    [divalproex label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=08a65cf4-7749-4ceb-6895-8f4805e2b01f).

## Why blood tests are sometimes needed
Not every seizure medicine needs blood work. Several do, and there are four reasons a doctor
orders it.

1. To measure the drug level. Phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate and phenobarbital have a
   fairly narrow range where they work well without causing trouble. A level helps explain a
   breakthrough seizure or an unexpected side effect.
2. To watch the organs that process the drug. Liver enzymes, blood counts, and sodium are
   the usual checks.
3. To sort out an interaction. When another medicine is added or stopped, an ASM level can
   move even though nothing about the dose changed.
4. Before starting, in a few cases. Carbamazepine labeling calls for genetic screening for
   a variant called HLA-B\*1502 in people with ancestry from broad areas of Asia, because that
   variant raises the risk of a severe skin reaction.

A level on its own does not decide anything. Doctors treat the person, not the number. Somebody
can be seizure free with a level below the usual range, and somebody else can have side effects
inside it.
([Johannessen Landmark et al., *Epileptic Disorders* 2023](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/epd2.20069);
[carbamazepine label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=8d409411-aa9f-4f3a-a52c-fbcb0c3ec053))

## Generic against brand
A generic must contain the same active ingredient in the same amount as the brand, and the FDA
requires it to be shown bioequivalent before approval.
([FDA, Generic Drug Facts](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/generic-drugs/generic-drug-facts)) Most
people with epilepsy do fine on generics, and the price difference is not small. A month of
generic levetiracetam costs a Minnesota pharmacy about $4.43; the brand costs about $595. See
[Medication Access](../../initiatives/medication-access.md) for the full price picture.

So why is anyone told to stay on one version? Because small differences do exist between
manufacturers, in the inactive ingredients and in how fast the pill dissolves. For most drugs
that does not matter. For a person whose seizure control sits on a knife edge, some
neurologists judge that it might. The Epilepsy Foundation's practical advice is to ask the
pharmacist to supply the same manufacturer at every refill, so the version does not change
without anyone noticing.
([Epilepsy Foundation](https://www.epilepsy.com/treatment/medicines/common-concerns))

If your prescription says "dispense as written" or "no substitution," that is deliberate. Ask
your neurologist why before you agree to a switch at the counter, and ask the pharmacy to call
the prescriber if the usual version is out of stock.

## What to do about a missed dose
Ask your prescriber for a missed-dose plan now, before you need one, and write it on the
prescription bottle or in your phone. The right answer depends on the drug and on how long
ago the dose was due. Do not double up to catch up unless you have been told to.

Two pieces of research together make the picture clear.

A prospective study published in *Annals of Neurology* in 2026 followed adults with
drug-resistant epilepsy for ten months each, recording every dose and every seizure through
smartphone apps. Missing a dose the day before showed no measurable link to having a seizure
(p = 0.68). The authors concluded that clinicians can reassure people that an occasional brief
lapse is unlikely to trigger a seizure right away.
([Goldenholz et al., 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41489031/);
[full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12782288/))

Sustained gaps are a different matter. In 33,658 adults with epilepsy on Medicaid, the stretches
when people were not taking their medicine carried about three times the death rate, one and a
half times the emergency visits, and twice the rate of injury-causing crashes. See
[Medication Access](../../initiatives/medication-access.md) for that analysis.

One forgotten dose is not a crisis. Running out for a week is. Treat them differently, and tell
your doctor honestly how often doses get missed, because a pattern may mean the schedule needs
to change rather than the person.

## Rescue medicines
A rescue medicine is not a daily medicine. It is given during a seizure that has gone on too
long, or during a cluster of seizures, to stop it before it becomes an emergency. All of the
approved ones are benzodiazepines, and all can be given by a family member, a school nurse, or
a trained staff member outside a hospital.

Three are FDA approved for out-of-hospital use:

- Diazepam rectal gel (Diastat), approved from age 2
- Diazepam nasal spray (Valtoco), approved from age 6
- Midazolam nasal spray (Nayzilam), approved from age 12

([Epilepsy Foundation, rescue medications](https://www.epilepsy.com/recognition/rescue-medications);
[nasal rescue medicines](https://www.epilepsy.com/treatment/seizure-rescue-therapies/nasal-rescue-medicines))

When to use one, and whether to call 911 afterward, is written into the person's
[seizure action plan](../04-seizure-action-plans/index.md) by their provider. If a student has
a rescue medicine, the school needs a copy of that plan, a place to store the medicine, and at
least one trained adult on every shift.
[For Schools: In Practice](../08-for-schools-in-practice/index.md) covers how districts set
that up.

## Interactions worth asking about
Bring a full list of everything to every appointment, including things that do not feel like
medicine.

- Hormonal birth control. Enzyme-inducing seizure medicines, including carbamazepine,
  phenytoin, phenobarbital and primidone, speed up the breakdown of the hormones in the pill,
  patch and ring, which can make them fail. It runs the other way too: the estrogen in combined
  pills lowers lamotrigine levels, which can mean breakthrough seizures when the pill starts and
  side effects during the hormone-free week. Hormonal IUDs and barrier methods are not affected
  the same way. ACOG advises that the neurologist and the gynecologist coordinate before
  anything changes.
  ([ACOG Committee Opinion 806](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/committee-opinion/articles/2020/05/gynecologic-management-of-adolescents-and-young-women-with-seizure-disorders))
- Pregnancy, and planning for it. Valproate carries a boxed warning for birth defects and
  reduced IQ in children exposed before birth. This is a conversation to have long before anyone
  is pregnant, because switching takes months and stopping abruptly is dangerous.
  ([FDA label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=08a65cf4-7749-4ceb-6895-8f4805e2b01f))
- Other prescriptions, especially antibiotics, antifungals, heart medicines, and
  antidepressants.
- Over-the-counter medicines and supplements. St. John's wort is the classic problem. Ask
  before adding anything, including melatonin and CBD products bought off a shelf.
- Alcohol and recreational drugs. Covered in
  [Everyday Safety](../09-everyday-safety/index.md).

## Questions to take to the neurologist
Print these or put them in your phone. Ask them at the first appointment and again whenever a
medicine changes.

1. What is this medicine called, and is it for my seizure type?
2. What side effects should I expect in the first month, and which ones mean I should call you?
3. What do I do if I miss a dose?
4. Does this interact with anything else I take, including birth control and supplements?
5. Do I need blood tests, and how often?
6. Is it safe for me to switch between generic manufacturers, or should the prescription say
   no substitution?
7. How long until we know whether this one is working?
8. If it does not work, what is the next step?
9. Do I need a rescue medicine, and who needs to be trained to give it?
10. What should my school have on file, and who signs it?
11. Can I drive, swim, or play my sport while we work this out?
12. If I ever want to come off this medicine, how would that be done?

Write the answers down in the appointment. Nobody remembers them afterward.

## Check yourself

??? quiz "1. Your first seizure medicine has not stopped the seizures. What does the evidence say about the second one?"
    It is worth trying. In a 30-year cohort, 45.7% became seizure free on the first drug and
    another 11.6% on the second. Roughly nine in ten of everyone who reached seizure freedom
    did so on the first or second medicine.

??? quiz "2. Side effects are bothering you. What is the one thing not to do?"
    Stop the medicine yourself. Abrupt withdrawal can cause worse seizures, including status
    epilepticus. Call the prescriber and describe what you are feeling; a dose change or a
    different drug usually solves it.

??? quiz "3. You forgot last night's dose. Emergency?"
    No. A 2026 prospective study found no measurable link between missing a dose the day
    before and having a seizure. Follow the missed-dose plan your prescriber gave you, and
    tell them if it is happening often, because repeated gaps are the dangerous pattern.
