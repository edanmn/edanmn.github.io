# 13. Tests and Diagnosis

Someone has had a seizure. Now there are appointments, a machine with wires, and a wait for
answers. This chapter explains what each test is for, what it can and cannot tell you, and how
to walk in prepared.

!!! warning "Read this first"
    This is general information, not medical advice. Only a licensed clinician can diagnose
    epilepsy or interpret a test. This page was written by students from public sources and has
    not yet been reviewed by a clinician or the Epilepsy Foundation of Minnesota. We are seeking
    that review. In an emergency, call 911.

## The story matters most
People expect the machines to give the answer. They usually do not. The diagnosis rests mainly
on the history: what happened, in what order, and what it looked like to somebody watching.

That is why the witness account carries so much weight. The person having a seizure often
remembers nothing, or remembers only the start. A neurologist wants to hear from whoever was
in the room. The Epilepsy Foundation puts it plainly: a description of the event from you and
anyone who saw it is probably the most important information the neurologist has.
([Epilepsy Foundation](https://www.epilepsy.com/diagnosis/eeg/what-if-its-normal))

So the most useful thing you can do before the appointment costs nothing.

- Write it down now, while it is fresh, and ask the witness to write their own version.
- Record the next one on a phone if it is safe to do so and someone else is handling first
  aid. Video answers questions that words cannot.
- Note the time. When it started, how long it lasted, how long until the person was fully
  back to normal. People routinely overestimate length, and the number matters.
- Note what came before. Sleep the night before, missed meals, illness, fever, a new
  medicine, alcohol, a head injury, flashing lights, stress.

## What to write down before the appointment
Bring this on paper. You will not remember it in the room.

- A timeline of every event, including older ones that seemed like nothing: staring spells,
  odd smells or tastes, sudden fear, jerks on waking, brief confusion.
- Every medicine, supplement, vitamin, and over-the-counter product, with doses.
- Birth and developmental history for a child, including any febrile seizures.
- Family history of seizures, epilepsy, or unexplained fainting.
- Any head injury, meningitis, encephalitis, or stroke.
- Past test results and records from other clinics, requested in advance so they arrive in time.
- Your questions, written out, with space to write the answers.

## What to bring to the visit
Insurance card, photo ID, the written history above, the videos, a list of pharmacies you use,
and a second adult if possible. One person asks questions, the other takes notes. For a child,
bring snacks, a favorite item, and something to do.

## EEG
An electroencephalogram records the brain's electrical activity. Small metal discs go on
the scalp with paste, wires run to a machine, and the person lies still while it records. A
routine EEG takes about 20 to 40 minutes plus setup. It does not hurt and delivers nothing
into the body; it only listens.

During the recording the technologist may ask you to breathe fast for a few minutes or shine a
flashing light at you. Both can bring out abnormal patterns.

What an EEG can show. Spikes and sharp waves between seizures, called interictal
epileptiform discharges, which support an epilepsy diagnosis. Where in the brain those patterns
start. Which seizure type is likely, which helps the neurologist choose a medicine.

What an EEG cannot show. It cannot prove epilepsy is absent, and an abnormal squiggle on
its own does not prove it is present.

!!! note "A normal EEG does not rule out epilepsy"
    An EEG records only the brain activity happening during the test. Between seizures the
    brain usually looks normal. About half of all EEGs done for people with seizures are read
    as normal, and someone who has seizures every week can still have a normal recording.
    ([Epilepsy Foundation](https://www.epilepsy.com/diagnosis/eeg/what-if-its-normal))

    After a first unprovoked seizure, a pooled analysis found routine EEG picked up
    epileptiform changes in 17.3% of adults and 57.8% of children.
    ([Bouma et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26073548/))

    If the first EEG is normal and the story still points to seizures, the next step is usually
    a longer or repeated recording, often sleep-deprived or including sleep, because that raises
    the chance of catching something. Do not read a normal result as being told nothing
    happened.

### Preparing a child for an EEG
- Wash the hair the night before. Skip conditioner, oils, gel, and hairspray, because they stop
  the discs from sticking.
- Ask the clinic whether to keep the child up late or wake them early. Many EEGs are ordered
  sleep-deprived on purpose, so sleep is part of the test rather than a failure of it.
- Explain honestly and simply: stickers on the head, a hat of wires, no shots, no pain. Comparing
  it to a photograph of the brain helps. Let them watch a short video of a child having one.
- Bring a comfort item, a blanket, a bottle, or a tablet for the wait.
- Plan for goopy hair afterward. The paste washes out with warm water and regular shampoo, though
  it may take two goes.
- Ask whether medicines should be taken as usual that morning. Usually yes, but ask.

## Video EEG monitoring
If a routine EEG does not settle the question, the next step may be an admission to an
epilepsy monitoring unit, or EMU. This is a hospital room where EEG runs continuously
alongside a video camera, so the recording of the brain and the recording of the body line up
second by second.

What an admission is like:

- Length. Usually one to seven days, commonly four to seven. It depends on how long it takes
  to capture events.
- You stay in the room. The wires are hardwired to the wall, so you can move around the room
  but not leave it. Bring what you need for several days.
- Someone watches around the clock. Video, EEG, and nursing staff, all continuously.
- The team may try to provoke seizures on purpose. Medicines are often reduced, and sleep
  deprivation, flashing lights, or exercise may be used. This sounds alarming. It is done in the
  one place designed to handle it safely, because a seizure that nobody records answers nothing.
- Press the button. Patients and families are asked to push an event button and then say out
  loud what they are feeling. That marker is gold to the person reading the recording afterward.

What it answers: whether these events are epileptic seizures at all, which matters because some
events that look like seizures are not; which type they are; where in the brain they start; and
whether epilepsy surgery is worth considering.
([Epilepsy Foundation, video EEG](https://www.epilepsy.com/diagnosis/eeg/video-eeg) and
[what to expect in the EMU (PDF)](https://www.epilepsy.com/sites/default/files/2023-11/what-to-expect-EMU-english.pdf);
[American Academy of Pediatrics](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/epilepsy/diagnosing-pediatric-epilepsy/what-to-expect-in-an-epilepsy-monitoring-unit/))

Pack for a hotel stay with no shower: loose button-front tops that go on over wires, your own
pillow, chargers with long cords, schoolwork, headphones, and something to do that survives
several dull days.

## MRI
An MRI is a detailed picture of the brain's structure. It looks for a physical cause of the
seizures: a scar, a malformation in how part of the brain formed, a tumor, damage from an old
stroke or injury. Epilepsy centers use a specific "epilepsy protocol" MRI with thin slices
through the areas seizures most often come from, which is not the same as a standard brain MRI.

Practical points:

- It is loud and takes 30 to 60 minutes. Ear protection is provided.
- No metal in the room. Tell the team about implants, braces, or a seizure-detection device.
- Young children and some adults need sedation to stay still, which the clinic arranges in
  advance.
- The CT scan done in the emergency room is not a substitute. CT is fast and good at finding
  bleeding. MRI is better at finding the causes of epilepsy.

An MRI finding also changes the odds. The AAN and the American Epilepsy Society found that a
significant abnormality on brain imaging raises the risk of a second seizure, as does an
epileptiform EEG.
([AAN/AES guideline, *Neurology* 2015](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000001487);
[NINDS](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/health-information/disorders/epilepsy-and-seizures))

## Blood tests
Blood work after a first seizure is doing two jobs.

Looking for a cause that is not epilepsy. A seizure triggered by something temporary is
called a provoked seizure, and it does not mean a person has epilepsy. Tests check sodium,
glucose, calcium, magnesium, kidney and liver function, blood counts, signs of infection, and
sometimes toxins or drug levels.

Setting a baseline. If a seizure medicine is going to be started, the team wants to know
what the liver, kidneys, blood counts, and sodium looked like beforehand. Some medicines also
call for genetic screening before the first dose, as described in
[Medicines for Epilepsy](../12-medicines-for-epilepsy/index.md).

Genetic testing is a separate thing and is more common in children, in epilepsy that starts very
young, and where there is developmental delay or a family pattern. It is ordered when it might
change the treatment, not routinely.

## What the results mean for a diagnosis
The International League Against Epilepsy defines epilepsy as any one of three situations:

1. Two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart.
2. One unprovoked seizure plus a risk of another that is at least 60%, which is where the EEG
   and MRI findings come in.
3. Diagnosis of a specific epilepsy syndrome.

([Fisher et al., *Epilepsia* 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/epi.12550);
[ILAE plain-language summary](https://www.ilae.org/guidelines/definition-and-classification/the-2014-definition-of-epilepsy-a-perspective-for-patients-and-caregivers))

After a single unprovoked seizure, the risk of a second one within two years runs roughly 21%
to 45%, highest in the first year. Normal EEG and normal MRI push that estimate toward the low
end.
([AAN/AES guideline](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000001487))

Three things to hold onto. Normal tests do not mean nothing happened. A diagnosis can take
months and more than one round of testing, and that is ordinary rather than a sign of poor care.
And "we do not know the cause" is a common, honest answer that does not stop treatment from
working.

Once there is a diagnosis, the school side starts. See
[Seizure Action Plans](../04-seizure-action-plans/index.md) for the document the school needs and
[Your Child's Rights](../07-your-childs-rights/index.md) for 504 plans and IEPs.

## Where this care happens in Minnesota
The National Association of Epilepsy Centers accredits epilepsy centers by level. Level 4 is
the highest: full video EEG monitoring, complex medication management, and epilepsy surgery.
Minnesota has four. They are Mayo Clinic in Rochester, the University of Minnesota Medical
Center, Abbott Northwestern in Minneapolis, and Minnesota Epilepsy Group with Children's
Minnesota in the Twin Cities.
([NAEC directory](https://naec-epilepsy.org/find-a-center))

All four sit in Rochester or the Twin Cities, which is a long way from much of the state. We
measured how far. 101 Minnesota school districts, roughly 70,000 students, are more than 60
miles from the nearest child neurologist, and 11 are more than 100 miles away. Look up any
district and see the full map on our [Distance to Care](../../initiatives/care-access.md) page.

If you live far from a center, ask the clinic about tele-neurology visits, whether EEG can be
done locally and read remotely, and whether Medical Assistance covers travel. See
[Resources & Support](../../resources/index.md).

## Check yourself

??? quiz "1. The EEG came back normal. Does that mean it was not a seizure?"
    No. About half of EEGs done for people with seizures read as normal, because the test only
    captures the brain activity during the recording. The history and the witness account still
    carry the most weight, and a longer or repeated EEG is often the next step.

??? quiz "2. Why would a hospital lower someone's seizure medicine on purpose during a video EEG stay?"
    To capture a seizure on camera and on EEG at the same time. That recording is what tells
    the team whether the events are epileptic, which type they are, and where they start. It is
    done in a monitored unit built to handle it.

??? quiz "3. What is the single most useful thing to bring to a first neurology appointment?"
    A written account from whoever saw the event, with times, plus any phone video. The person
    having the seizure usually cannot describe it, and the description drives the diagnosis.
