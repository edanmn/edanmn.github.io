# 14. Mental Health and Epilepsy

Epilepsy is hard on the mind as well as the body. Depression and anxiety show up far more often in
people with epilepsy than in people without it, and they are often missed. That matters, because
both are treatable. This chapter is about noticing the problem early and knowing where to take it.

!!! danger "Need help right now?"
    If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

    - 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. Call or text 988, or chat at
      [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/). Free, 24 hours a day, every day. Text and chat
      are also available in Spanish.
    - Crisis Text Line. Text HOME to 741741 to reach a trained crisis counselor.
      Texting HOLA reaches a Spanish-speaking counselor.
    - Your county's mobile crisis team. Minnesota counties run 24/7 crisis teams that can come
      to where you are. Look up your county's number on the Minnesota Department of Human Services
      crisis page,
      [mn.gov/dhs crisis contacts](https://mn.gov/dhs/people-we-serve/adults/health-care/mental-health/resources/crisis-contacts.jsp).
      If you cannot find it quickly, call 988 and they will route you.
    - Find a provider. [Fast-Tracker](https://fasttrackermn.org/) is a free Minnesota search
      tool that lists mental health and substance use providers with current openings.
    - At school. Your school counselor, social worker, psychologist, or nurse can help the same
      day. You do not need an appointment to knock on the door.
    - Epilepsy Foundation of Minnesota. Free Connect Groups for teens, adults, and caregivers,
      plus one-to-one support: [epilepsyfoundationmn.org/connect](https://www.epilepsyfoundationmn.org/connect/),
      651-287-2300.

    This page is general information, not medical advice, and it does not name or recommend any

## How common is it

Mood and anxiety problems are the most common non-seizure part of epilepsy.

- A meta-analysis of 27 studies found that about 22.9% of people with epilepsy had a depressive
  disorder and about 20.2% had an anxiety disorder.[^scott2017] That is roughly one in four and
  one in five.
- An earlier review of population studies put active depression at 23.1%, about 2.7 times the
  rate in people without epilepsy.[^mula2021]
- In children and teenagers with epilepsy, pooled estimates were about 18.9% for anxiety and
  13.5% for depression.[^scott2020]
- The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke puts it plainly: in adults with
  epilepsy, depression and anxiety are the two most frequent mental health diagnoses.[^ninds]

And it is undertreated. A CDC-published survey study found that among adults with epilepsy who had
serious psychological distress, 84% said they needed mental health care in the past year, but only
57% had actually seen a mental health provider.[^thompson2012] A 2021 review in *Neurology: Clinical
Practice* reached the same conclusion: these problems "are still underdiagnosed and
undertreated."[^mula2021]

So if you feel like something is off and nobody has asked you about it, that is a common experience,
not a sign that you are imagining things.

## Why it happens

There is no single reason, and you do not have to pick one.

- The condition itself. The parts of the brain involved in many seizures, especially in the
  temporal lobe, overlap with the parts that handle mood and fear. The link runs both ways:
  depression roughly doubles a person's later risk of developing epilepsy, which suggests shared
  biology rather than one thing simply causing the other.[^mula2021]
- The social load. Epilepsy still carries stigma. Add missed school, not being able to drive
  yet, losing some independence, and explaining yourself over and over, and the weight adds
  up.[^mula2021]
- Sometimes the medicine. About one in six people recently started on an antiseizure medicine
  develops some psychiatric side effect.[^mula2021] More on this below.
- Poor sleep. Sleep problems are common in epilepsy, and bad sleep feeds both seizures and low
  mood.[^ninds]

None of these make the feelings less real, and none of them mean you have to just cope.

## What to watch for in a teenager

Teenagers do not usually announce that they are depressed. Adults often miss it because normal teen
moodiness and real depression can look alike at a glance. The difference is usually how long it
lasts and how much it takes away.

Worth paying attention to when it goes on for two weeks or more:

- Dropping the things they used to like. Quitting a sport, a club, or a group chat, and not
  replacing it with anything.
- Irritability and anger rather than sadness. In teens, this is often what depression looks like
  from the outside.
- Sleeping much more or much less than usual, and a big change in appetite.
- Grades sliding, more missed school, more time alone in a room.
- Physical complaints with no clear cause: headaches, stomachaches, always tired.
- Anxiety that has taken over specific situations. Refusing to sleep over at a friend's house,
  avoiding the cafeteria, panicking about having a seizure in public.
- Fear of the next seizure that is running the person's life. This is common and it has a name in
  the literature: seizure-related anxiety.
- Any talk about not wanting to be here, giving things away, or "everyone would be better off."
  Take this seriously the first time. Ask directly. Asking does not plant the idea.

If you are the teenager reading this: you are allowed to say "I am not okay" without having a tidy
explanation ready.

## Memory and concentration

Trouble with memory and focus is one of the most common complaints in epilepsy, and it is not a
character flaw. A third or more of people with epilepsy have cognitive or neuropsychiatric symptoms
that affect their quality of life.[^ninds] Seizures themselves, the after-effects that follow one,
some medicines, poor sleep, and anxiety all pull in the same direction.

Things that tend to help:

- Ask for a neuropsychological evaluation. This is a set of tests that shows exactly which parts
  of memory and attention are affected. It is the difference between "I'm bad at school" and a
  specific, fixable picture.
- Put the accommodations in writing. Extra time, a note-taker or copies of notes, tests in a
  quiet room, and permission to retake a test after a seizure are all standard. See
  [Your Child's Rights (504/IEP)](../07-your-childs-rights/index.md).
- Write things down and stop relying on memory. Phone reminders for medicine, one calendar for
  everything, photos of the whiteboard.
- Protect the hours right after a seizure. Studying during that window is usually wasted effort.
- Bring it up with the neurologist. If a medicine is the main problem, there are usually other
  options. Never change a dose on your own.

## Sleep

Sleep and seizures push on each other in both directions. Losing sleep is a powerful seizure
trigger, sleep disorders are common in people with epilepsy, and treating a sleep problem can reduce
seizures.[^ninds] Low mood and anxiety wreck sleep too, which closes the loop.

Practical version:

- Same bedtime and wake time, including weekends. This is the single highest-value habit.
- All-nighters carry a real seizure risk, so treat them as off the table.
- Keep a short log of bedtimes, wake times, nighttime events, and when medicine was taken. Patterns
  show up fast, and doctors can use it.
- If someone snores heavily, gasps at night, or is exhausted despite enough hours in bed, mention it
  to the doctor. Sleep apnea is treatable and it affects seizures.

## Antiseizure medicines and mood

Some antiseizure medicines carry a warning about mood and behavior. This is worth asking about
rather than guessing at.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration pooled 199 placebo-controlled trials of 11 antiseizure drugs.
Patients taking an antiseizure medicine had about twice the rate of suicidal thinking or behavior:
0.43% compared with 0.24% on placebo, which works out to about one extra case for every 530
people treated.[^fdalabel] Because of this, every antiseizure medicine label in the United States
carries a warning to watch for new or worsening depression, unusual changes in mood or behavior, and
thoughts of self-harm.

Two things follow from that, and they have to be held together:

1. The risk is small but real, and it is a good reason to ask your doctor "does this one affect mood,
   and what should we watch for?" Some medicines are more associated with irritability or mood
   changes than others.
2. Nobody should stop or change an antiseizure medicine on their own. Stopping suddenly can cause
   seizures, including prolonged ones, which is a much larger and more immediate danger. Bring the
   concern to the prescriber and change it with them.

If mood or behavior changed within weeks of starting or increasing a medicine, say so with the dates.
That timing is the most useful thing a doctor can hear.

## What to say to a doctor

Neurology appointments are short and tend to fill up with seizure counts. Mental health gets
squeezed out unless someone says it out loud. Open with it instead of saving it for the end.

Sentences that work:

- "Before we talk about seizures, I want to talk about my mood."
- "For the past two months I have not enjoyed anything, and I am sleeping a lot more."
- "I am anxious about having a seizure in public, and I have stopped going to things because of it."
- "I started this medicine in August and I have been much more irritable since. Could they be
  connected?"
- "Can I be screened for depression and anxiety, and can you refer me to someone who treats it?"
- "I forget things I should know. Can I get a neuropsychological evaluation?"

Before you go, write down three things you want answered and bring the list. Ask for a copy of the
plan you agree on. If an answer is brushed off and the problem does not go away, it is reasonable to
ask again or to ask for a referral.

## Getting help in Minnesota

- Crisis, any hour: 988 by call or text, Crisis Text Line by texting HOME to 741741, your county's
  mobile crisis team, or 911 if someone is in immediate danger. All of these are listed in the box
  at the top of this page.
- Your school. Counselors, social workers, and school psychologists are free and already in the
  building. They can also connect a mental health need to a 504 plan or an IEP.
- Epilepsy Foundation of Minnesota. Free Connect Groups, including teen and young adult groups
  and a Spanish-language group, plus care coordination and community health workers. Talking to
  other people who get it does real work that a pamphlet cannot.
  [epilepsyfoundationmn.org/connect](https://www.epilepsyfoundationmn.org/connect/)
- Fast-Tracker. Free statewide search for therapists, psychiatry, and substance use services,
  with current availability: [fasttrackermn.org](https://fasttrackermn.org/)
- Your epilepsy team. Ask whether the clinic has a psychologist or social worker attached to it.
  Many epilepsy centers do, and that is usually the fastest route in.
- More options: [Resources & Support](../../resources/index.md) and
  [For Students](../../for-students/index.md).

## The short version

Depression and anxiety are common with epilepsy, they are treatable, and they are missed far too
often. Say something early, say it to a person who can act, and keep saying it until someone does.
