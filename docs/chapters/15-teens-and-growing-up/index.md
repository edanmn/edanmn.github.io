# 15. Teens and Growing Up with Epilepsy

This chapter is for you. Not for your parents, not for your teachers. You are going to be running
your own life soon, and epilepsy comes with you. Most of it is workable. Some of it takes planning.
Here is the honest version of both.

!!! warning "Before you read"
    This is general information, not medical advice, and there is nothing here about doses or which
    medicine to take. What is safe for you depends on your seizure type, how often you have
    seizures, and what your own doctor says. Like the rest of this site, this page was written by
    students and has not yet been reviewed by a clinician. In an emergency, call 911.

    If you are struggling with your mood, or you are in crisis, go to
    [Mental Health and Epilepsy](../14-mental-health-and-epilepsy/index.md). Call or text 988 any
    hour, or call 911 if you are in immediate danger.

## Telling people

You do not owe anyone your medical history. That is real, and it stays true your whole life. But
there is a practical reason to tell a few people anyway: if you have a seizure in front of someone
who knows what to do, it goes fine. If you have one in front of someone who panics, it goes badly.

Who actually needs to know:

- The school nurse and your teachers, through your seizure action plan. This is already set up
  in most cases.
- Your coach, if you play a sport.
- Two or three friends you are around a lot. Especially anyone you swim with, drive with, or
  stay over with.
- A supervisor or a coworker, if you work somewhere with machinery, heights, or water.

Who does not need to know: everyone else. You are allowed to have a private medical condition.

Telling someone takes about fifteen seconds:

> "I have epilepsy, so I sometimes have seizures. If it happens, don't panic. Don't hold me down and
> don't put anything in my mouth. Move stuff out of the way, roll me on my side, time it, and call
> 911 if it goes past 5 minutes. I'll be groggy after. Just stay with me."

Most people handle it fine. Some will ask awkward questions, and that is usually curiosity rather
than judgment. If someone treats you badly about it, that is information about them.

## Sports and activities

Start from yes. Exercise is good for you, and the evidence suggests physical activity and sport may
actually help seizure control on top of the usual benefits.[^ilae] The International League Against
Epilepsy sorts sports into three groups by what would happen if you had a seizure during them: no
extra risk, moderate risk to you but not to anyone else, and major risk.[^ilae]

Usually fine with a plan: basketball, soccer, track, cross country, volleyball, baseball and
softball, tennis, hockey, wrestling, football, dance, weight training with a spotter, most club
sports. Wear the normal gear everyone wears. Tell your coach what your seizures look like and what to
do. Having one teammate who knows means you are not managing it alone.

Needs supervision, specific rules, or a real conversation with your neurologist:

- Swimming. Never swim alone. Swim with a buddy who knows, in sight of a lifeguard you have
  told. A lifeguard watches the whole pool and does not count as your buddy. Open water needs a
  proper life jacket.
- Anything at height. Rock climbing without a rope, high diving, gymnastics on high apparatus,
  ladders and roofs at a job.
- Scuba diving, skydiving, hang gliding, bungee jumping. These are usually off the table if
  seizures are not controlled, and the rules for diving are strict.
- Riding. Horseback riding, motocross, and mountain biking on hard terrain sit in the middle.
  Helmet, and ride with someone.
- Showers over baths, and do not lock the bathroom door. Boring, but drowning is the biggest
  everyday risk in epilepsy.

If your seizures have been controlled for a long time, the list of things that need extra care gets
short. Ask your neurologist where you actually land instead of assuming the worst. More detail in
[Everyday Safety & Living](../09-everyday-safety/index.md).

## Alcohol

You will be at parties. Skipping this section would be useless to you, so here is the straight
version.

Alcohol matters more for you than for the people around you, for three reasons.

1. Alcohol is a seizure trigger, and so is alcohol withdrawal, which is what your brain
   experiences the morning after heavy drinking.[^ninds] The risk is not mostly during the drinking.
   It is during the crash.
2. The knock-on effects do the damage. Drinking a lot means sleeping badly, sleeping in, and
   missing a dose. Missed medicine plus lost sleep is one of the most reliable ways to have a
   seizure.
3. Antiseizure medicines and alcohol stack. Both are sedating, so you get hit harder than your
   friends do by the same amount, and you may not notice how impaired you are.

Some epilepsy syndromes, juvenile myoclonic epilepsy in particular, react to even small amounts.
Cannabis, vapes with nicotine, stimulants, and anything unlabeled from someone's older brother are
all worse bets. Cocaine, amphetamines, and MDMA can trigger seizures outright.

If you are going to drink anyway, the harm reduction is simple: take your medicine on schedule
regardless, do not skip sleep, stay hydrated, do not swim or get in a hot tub, and stay with people
who know you have epilepsy. Have that conversation with your doctor honestly. Doctors have heard it
before and they are not calling your parents over it.

## Sleep and late nights

Sleep loss is one of the most reliable seizure triggers there is.[^ninds] That makes all-nighters a
different proposition for you than for your friends. A tired classmate does badly on a test. You
might have a seizure.

- Pick a bedtime and hold it, including weekends. Shifting your sleep by four hours on Saturday is
  its own kind of sleep deprivation.
- Finish the assignment at 11pm and hand in what you have rather than staying up until 3am. It is
  genuinely the better trade.
- Long gaming sessions, closing shifts, and sleepovers all cost sleep. You can do them. Just do not
  stack three of them in a week.
- If you are exhausted despite sleeping enough hours, tell your doctor. Sleep apnea and other sleep
  disorders are common with epilepsy and they are treatable.[^ninds]

## Driving

Driving is achievable, and for most people with epilepsy it is a question of when rather than
whether. In Minnesota, you generally need to be seizure-free for 3 months, plus a form signed by
both you and your doctor. There are follow-up certifications after that, and the exact rules can
change, so confirm the current requirements with your doctor and Minnesota Driver and Vehicle
Services rather than with a friend who heard something.

The full Minnesota rules are in
[Driving in Minnesota](../10-driving-in-minnesota/index.md).

Two things people do not tell you:

- Watching your friends get licenses first is one of the worst parts of teenage epilepsy. It is
  fair to be angry about it. It is also usually temporary.
- Learn the alternatives well. Transit, rideshare, biking, and a reliable circle of people who
  will drive you. Independence is the goal, and a license is only one route to it.

## Jobs and whether you have to disclose

Short answer: usually no, not when you apply.

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, an employer may not ask you about a disability before
making you a job offer. They can ask whether you are able to do the job. After a job offer, an
employer may require a medical exam if they require it of everyone in that role, and they cannot
withdraw the offer just because you disclosed epilepsy. They can only withdraw it if you cannot do
the essential parts of the job or you would pose a genuine, significant safety risk. Anything medical
you do tell them has to be kept confidential, and they may not announce it to your coworkers, even
if you have a seizure at work.[^eeoc]

Reasons people choose to tell anyway:

- You need an accommodation, like a schedule that protects your sleep, breaks to take medicine, or
  no solo shifts near machinery. Asking for an accommodation means disclosing.
- You want coworkers to know what to do if you have a seizure on shift. You can tell one manager and
  ask that it go no further, or brief a couple of coworkers yourself.
- Some jobs have rules you cannot get around: commercial driving, certain machinery, work at height.
  Better to know early.

It is your call, and it can be different at different jobs. If an employer treats you badly after
you disclose, that is potentially illegal, and the EEOC page linked below explains what you can do.

## Dating and independence

There is no trick to this. Tell someone you are dating once things are going somewhere, and tell
them before you are in a situation where it would matter, like a night away or a trip to a lake.
Keep it short and calm. How you say it sets how they take it.

Some practical notes:

- Anyone you stay overnight with should know what to do and when to call 911.
- Flashing lights at concerts and clubs only matter if you are photosensitive, which is about 3% of
  people with epilepsy. Most people with epilepsy are not. If you do not know, ask your neurologist
  instead of avoiding everything.
- Missed sleep and drinking are the parts of a relationship most likely to affect your seizures. Not
  romance.
- If someone treats your epilepsy as a burden or uses it to control what you do, that is a problem
  with the relationship.

## Running your own medicine

At some point this stops being your parent's job. Do it gradually and on purpose, rather than
discovering at college that you have never refilled a prescription.

- Learn the actual names and what they are for. Generic name, brand name, strength, and when you
  take them. Say them out loud until you can do it from memory at a pharmacy counter.
- Set alarms. Phone alarm at the same time every day, and a pillbox you fill on Sundays so you
  can see whether you took it.
- Learn what to do about a missed dose before you miss one. Ask your doctor or pharmacist now
  and write the answer down. Do not double up on your own.
- Watch the refill date, not the empty bottle. Call the pharmacy about a week out. Running out
  over a long weekend is a classic and avoidable way to have a seizure.
- Take over the pharmacy calls. Refills, insurance problems, prior authorizations. Do a few with
  a parent listening, then do them alone.
- Never stop or change a medicine on your own, even if you feel fine or you hate a side effect.
  Stopping suddenly can cause a seizure, including a long and dangerous one. Take the complaint to
  the prescriber, who usually has other options.

## What changes at 18

In Minnesota you are legally an adult at 18.[^mnadult] A few concrete things flip:

- You give consent for your own medical care. Your doctor talks to you. Decisions are yours.
- Your medical records become yours. Under the Minnesota Health Records Act you have the right to
  get copies of your own health records, and a provider generally needs your written authorization
  to release them to anyone else, including your parents.[^mnrecords] If you want your parents to
  keep helping, you sign a release saying so. That is a normal and easy thing to do.
- Insurance does not change at 18. You can usually stay on a parent's plan until 26. Find out
  now whose plan you are on and how to reach it.
- Pediatric neurology usually ends somewhere between 18 and 22. Ask your current team who they
  recommend for adult epilepsy care and get the referral before you need it, not after a seizure.
- Nobody automatically tells your college. If you want accommodations at college, you have to go
  to the disability services office yourself and bring documentation. A high school 504 plan does not
  travel with you.

Being an adult patient does not mean doing it alone. It means you are the one who decides who helps.

## Taking over talking to your own doctor

This is a skill, and it gets easier fast. Build it over a few appointments rather than all at once.

1. Appointment one: you answer the doctor's questions yourself while your parent stays quiet.
2. Appointment two: you bring three written questions and ask them.
3. Appointment three: ask your parent to step out for part of the visit. Most epilepsy clinics
   expect this and will offer it.
4. Appointment four: you book it, you show up with your own list and your own medicine list, and
   you leave knowing the plan.

Things to bring every time: your seizure log with dates and descriptions, your current medicines and
strengths, questions written down, and anything that changed since last visit including mood and
sleep. Things to ask: what type of epilepsy do I have, what should I avoid, what happens if I miss a
dose, how do I reach you between visits, and when can I be considered for driving.

If you want a script for talking about mood, there is one in
[Mental Health and Epilepsy](../14-mental-health-and-epilepsy/index.md#what-to-say-to-a-doctor).

[Got Transition](https://www.gottransition.org/youth-and-young-adults/) has free checklists for
exactly this handover.

## For parents: letting go safely

This section is short on purpose, because the chapter is not about you.

Your job shifts from doing it to backing it up. Teenagers who never practice managing epilepsy
become twenty-year-olds who cannot, and that is the more dangerous outcome. Some ways to make the
handover survivable:

- Hand over one thing at a time, with a deadline. Alarms this month, pharmacy calls next.
- Let small failures happen while the stakes are low. A refill left too late at 16, with you
  there as backup, teaches more than five reminders.
- Step out of part of the appointment starting around 14 or 15. Ask the clinic to talk to your
  teenager directly.
- Sign the releases at 18 rather than treating the legal change as a wall. Your teenager can
  authorize you to keep receiving information, and most are glad to.
- Negotiate rather than forbid. A blanket no to parties produces a teenager who goes anyway and
  tells you nothing. Agreeing on rules about medicine, sleep, water, and a friend who knows produces
  one who texts you.
- Ask about mood, not only seizures. Depression and anxiety are common in epilepsy and often go
  unnoticed. See [Mental Health and Epilepsy](../14-mental-health-and-epilepsy/index.md).
- Keep the non-negotiables short. Medicine on time, no swimming alone, tell someone where you
  are. Three rules that get followed beat twenty that do not.

The goal is an adult who runs their own epilepsy care and calls you because they want to.
