# On the Phone with 911

Deciding to call is the easy half. The hard half starts when someone picks up and asks you a
question you were not ready for. People freeze, or they lead with the panic instead of the
address, and the call takes twice as long as it needed to.

This simulator puts you on the phone. A dispatcher asks one question at a time and you choose
what to say. Vague or guessed answers get a reply explaining what that costs.

## The three calls

1. The seizure is still going. A student with epilepsy, four minutes in, rescue medicine
   already given. The straightforward version, done in order.
2. You do not know this student. You are a substitute on lunch duty. You cannot give an age
   or a seizure history, and the right answer is to say so and name who is finding out.
3. It stops while you are on the phone. A first seizure after a fall from the bleachers, and
   the jerking stops mid-call. You have to decide whether to cancel the ambulance.

## The order the call goes in

1. Address of the school, street and city, before anything else
2. Where in the building: room, floor, nearest outside door
3. What is happening, described plainly
4. How long it has been going on, as a clock time
5. Whether the person is breathing
6. Age, and whether they have had seizures before
7. What has been given, which medicine and at what time
8. Who is meeting the crew, and at which door
9. Stay on the line until the dispatcher releases you

## What it teaches that the first aid simulator does not

- Location comes first because it is the only thing that survives a dropped call.
- A guessed age or a vague maybe on medication gets typed into a record and handed to the
  hospital as fact. "I do not know, the office is checking" is a complete answer.
- Duration has to be a number. Watching a seizure stretches time, and nobody estimates it well.
- School doors lock. Somebody has to be standing at the right one before the siren arrives.
- The student's seizure action plan and the rescue medicine box should be in someone's hand when
  the crew walks in.
- A seizure stopping does not cancel the reasons you called. A first seizure or an injury still
  needs assessment, and standing a crew down is a decision made with the dispatcher.
- One person talks to dispatch and does nothing else. Someone else calls the family.

The simulator ends with a call card you can print and tape inside a cupboard door: the order of
information, and the list of times to write down once the ambulance has gone.

    Schools and addresses in the scenarios are fictional. A learning tool, not medical advice or certification. Dispatch protocols vary between
    counties, so follow what your own dispatcher tells you on the day. For free training in
