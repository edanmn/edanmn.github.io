# I was just diagnosed with epilepsy

Written for an adult in Minnesota, in the order things actually come up. Skip anything that
does not apply to you.

## This week

Do not stop or change any medicine on your own. Stopping suddenly is more dangerous than
most people expect. If a side effect is frightening you, call the prescriber's office today
and say so. There is almost always another option.

Find out whether you can drive. In Minnesota you have to report a loss of consciousness to
Driver and Vehicle Services within 30 days, and driving is usually cancelled until three months
have passed since the episode, with four specific exceptions written into the rule. This is the
question that upends people's lives fastest, so get the real answer rather than a rumour:
[driving in Minnesota](../../chapters/10-driving-in-minnesota/index.md).

Tell one or two people what to do. Someone at home, someone at work. Five minutes with
[seizure first aid](../../chapters/02-seizure-first-aid/index.md) is enough. The thing people
get wrong most often is holding someone down or putting something in their mouth.

Start a seizure diary. Date, time, how long, what you were doing, how much you had slept,
whether you missed a dose. Paper or your phone, it does not matter. This is the single most
useful thing you can bring to the next appointment.

## This month

Know what to ask about your medicine, and what it costs here. Questions for the prescriber,
and what Minnesotans actually pay:
[medicines: what to ask and what they cost](../../chapters/12-medicines-for-epilepsy/index.md).

Get ready for appointments. What to bring, what to ask, and how far specialist care is from
where you live: [appointments and tests](../../chapters/13-tests-and-diagnosis/index.md).

Sort out the money before it becomes a problem. Prior authorizations, the 72-hour emergency
supply, and what to do if a pharmacy quotes you a number you cannot pay:
[paying for epilepsy care](../../chapters/11-paying-for-epilepsy-care/index.md).

Think about sleep and alcohol honestly. Both matter more now than they did. Nobody is
telling you to become a different person, but a run of four-hour nights is a real risk factor.

## Before your next appointment

Bring the diary. Bring a witness account if anyone saw a seizure, because you cannot describe
what you were not conscious for. Then ask:

- What type of seizures do I have, and what does that mean for me?
- What are we hoping this medicine does, and by when will we know?
- What should I do if I have another seizure? Do I need rescue medicine?
- What are the side effects worth calling about?
- What is my driving situation, in writing?
- Is there anything about alcohol, sleep or my other medicines I should change?
- If this medicine does not work, what is the next step?

## What does not change

Most people with epilepsy work, study, have relationships and raise children. About two thirds
become seizure free on medication. You are allowed to be angry about this for a while, and you
are allowed to need help with that: [mental health and epilepsy](../../chapters/14-mental-health-and-epilepsy/index.md).

## If your seizures do not stop

If two medicines have failed, ask whether you are considered drug resistant and whether you should be evaluated at an
epilepsy center. What that means, and how far the nearest one is:
[when the first medicines do not work](../../chapters/16-when-the-first-medicine-does-not-work/index.md).
