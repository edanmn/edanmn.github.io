# You have epilepsy. Now what?

This is written to you, not to your parents. It assumes you are smart and that you are tired of
being talked about in the third person while you are sitting right there.

## The five things worth knowing first

Most people will never notice. Around two thirds of people with epilepsy become seizure free
on medication. This is a condition you manage, not an identity you have to take on.

Sleep is the one that gets people. Missing sleep is one of the most common triggers, and
late nights plus early practice is exactly the pattern that sets it off. This is annoying and it
is also true.

Alcohol interacts with your medicine, not just with you. The rules are different for you
than for your friends, and it is worth knowing why before you are in the situation:
[teens and growing up](../../chapters/15-teens-and-growing-up/index.md).

Driving has actual rules in Minnesota. You report a loss of consciousness within 30 days,
and driving is usually cancelled for three months from the episode, with specific exceptions.
Find out where you stand rather than guessing:
[driving in Minnesota](../../chapters/10-driving-in-minnesota/index.md).

You can do almost every sport. A few things around water and height need a spotter. Ask your
neurologist about your situation rather than accepting a blanket no from someone who is
guessing.

## Telling people

You do not owe anyone your medical history. You do owe yourself at least one person in each
place you spend time who knows what to do. One friend at school, one at practice. Show them the
[four steps](../../now/index.md). It takes a minute, and it means nobody panics or calls an
ambulance you do not need.

## Taking over your own care

Somewhere around 16 or 17 is the right time to start doing this yourself, while your parents
are still around to catch anything you drop.

- Know the name and dose of your medicine without looking it up
- Refill the prescription yourself before it runs out
- Talk in your own appointments. Ask your parents to let you answer first
- Keep your own seizure diary
- Know what your seizures look like from the outside, because you cannot see them

At 18 the medical decisions and the records become legally yours. That is worth preparing for
rather than discovering: [what changes at 18](../../chapters/15-teens-and-growing-up/index.md).

## If you are struggling

Depression and anxiety are more common in people with epilepsy, and in teenagers specifically
they get missed a lot. It is not a character failure and it is treatable.
[Mental health and epilepsy](../../chapters/14-mental-health-and-epilepsy/index.md).

If you are in crisis right now: call or text 988, or text HOME to 741741. If someone is in
immediate danger, call 911.

## If you want to do something about it

EDAN is run by high school students. We audited every school district in Minnesota for seizure
plans, and we are meeting a legislator in November about what epilepsy medicine costs families.
If you want to work on that, email edanmnorg@gmail.com. We are not a support group, we are a
project, and there is plenty to do.
